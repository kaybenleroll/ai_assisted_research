- **Reader:** quantitatively literate researcher or analyst using ecology to learn the methods who can read basic R, interpret averages and uncertainty, and wants to choose and defend models for non-Gaussian responses.
- **Prerequisites:** algebra, functions and logs, elementary probability, derivatives and matrix notation; supply a short navigable refresher where these are used, including likelihood and conditional expectation. No prior GLM assumed.
- **Problem and outcome:** observations can be counts, proportions, positive measurements, or clustered/zero-heavy outcomes; ordinary Gaussian errors or an unexamined transformation can imply impossible predictions or wrong uncertainty. Readers should turn a question and sampling design into an estimand, likelihood or estimating equation, design matrix and link; fit, diagnose, compare and interpret models; identify what the data cannot establish; and explain defensible alternatives.
- **Scope and depth:** a substantial self-contained account from regression through derivation and ecological application, not a short survey. Teach quasi-likelihood, negative binomial, generalised linear mixed models, GAMs, multinomial and ordinal responses, zero-inflated/hurdle and Bayesian GLMs with actual models, fits, interpretation and failure cases. No length cap; size follows the reader outcomes and evidence, with no word estimate serving as an upper bound. Distinguish a strict GLM from its extensions.
- **Exclusions:** full causal inference, complete spatial/temporal modelling, full occupancy/detection modelling, advanced multivariate ordination, survival analysis, experimental design theory, asymptotic proofs in full generality, and comprehensive software/package surveys. Explain where these become necessary. Illustrations are not causal identification merely because fitted coefficients are adjusted.
- **Languages:** R is the main narrative and executable implementation. Reproduce the principal Gaussian, binomial, Poisson/offset, Gamma, NB and key extension examples in Python and Julia where supported, checking comparable quantities and explicitly identifying differences in parameterisation, fitting and uncertainty; full parity is not promised; do not imply all ecosystems share identical algorithms or features. Candidate R tools include base R, glmmTMB, mgcv and brms; Python GAM support and Julia MixedModels dispersion-family GLMM limitations require feasibility checks. Environment, versions, support and comparison tolerances remain unresolved. Use reproducible seeds and pinned inputs; simulations demonstrate mechanisms, real data demonstrate analytic judgement, not known ground truth.
- **Design:** one ecological sampling story follows the decisions from study question to revised model and communication, with linked synthetic data allowing known truth and deliberately introduced failures. Real, openly documented ecological data are required; candidates and licences/provenance, observations, response, units, effort, design and limitations remain unresolved. Do not pretend one dataset supports every distribution: use smaller coherent companion cases for genuinely different outcomes and state when an example changes. Candidate real studies, pending verification: Palmer Penguins for body mass, categorical contrasts and missingness (publisher indicates CC0); USGS Breeding Bird Survey release DOI 10.5066/P9J6QUF6 for repeat-route species counts (publisher indicates CC0). Raw BBS layout, zeros and end-year inconsistency remain unverified; do not invent effort or assume zeros mean observed absence. Dataset and licence evidence is in [the companion sources report](glm-primer.sources.md); [the case contract](glm-primer.case.md) records stage modes and acceptance.
- **Notation:** place a symbol/meaning/first-use table before first maths and define notation locally; follow `maintenance/docs/math-notation.md`, including transpose `^{\top}` and matrix/vector distinctions. Show assumptions and domains for displayed results.

## Ordered parts and route spines

The same decisions recur in each route: what was observed and sampled; what is predicted and for whom; how the mean and variability arise; how design enters; how fitting and uncertainty work; what evidence could reveal failure. Later routes modify a named assumption of an earlier model and feed their results back to the question, avoiding a catalogue of distributions.

| route_id | tier | Part and reader need | Organising model, application and boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| R01 | primary | I. From ecological question to regression | Sampling unit, estimand and ordinary regression; Gaussian continuous outcome with continuous and categorical covariates. Compare fitted contrasts and predictive intervals; dependent units, selection and confounding limit claims. |
| R02 | primary | II. GLM machinery | Distribution, mean, link and linear predictor; derive exponential-family moments, likelihood score, information and IRLS on Gaussian/logistic/Poisson cases. Numerical convergence and model assumptions matter. |
| R03 | primary | III. Probabilities and counts | Binomial trials/logit and Poisson events/log link; success/trial denominators, exposure offset, fitted probabilities/rates, interactions. Rare events, separation, exposure and independence failures expose limits. |
| R04 | primary | IV. Positive outcomes and inference | Gamma/log model for strictly positive skewed ecological response; likelihood, intervals, tests, prediction and residual diagnostics across families. Zeros and inverse-link averaging require care. |
| R05 | alternative | V. Dispersion and count alternatives | Overdispersed counts: quasi score with variance-scale uncertainty and separately motivated sandwich covariance, compared with a negative-binomial probability model; simulated and real count comparisons. Quasi does not specify a full likelihood; NB form and parameterisation must be explicit. |
| R06 | alternative | VI. Dependence | GLMM random intercept/slopes for clustered sampling; compare conditional/marginal predictions and prediction for new groups; few groups and weakly identified variance components limit inference. |
| R10 | alternative | VI. Flexible effects | GAM basis and penalty for nonlinear ecological gradients, with REML smoothing selection; fit, interpret and check a smooth; concurvity and extrapolation limit conclusions. |
| R11 | alternative | IV. Multicategory responses | Model unordered ecological categories using multinomial regression and ordered outcomes using an ordinal cumulative-link model; fit, interpret and compare probabilities, check proportional odds, and examine sparse categories and dependence. |
| R07 | alternative | VI. Excess zeros and observation process | Hurdle versus zero-inflated count generative stories; fit and decompose probabilities, compare predictions; zeros alone cannot identify mechanism, and imperfect detection is not automatically zero inflation. |
| R08 | alternative | VII. Bayesian modelling and decisions | Prior, likelihood and posterior for binomial and count models, prior/posterior predictive checks and predictive intervals; compare frequentist estimates under explicit priors. Separation, prior sensitivity, sampling diagnostics and computation matter. |
| R09 | reference-only | Beyond the central route | Beta and Tweedie response models, full occupancy/detection, spatial/time-series and survival modelling: explain the different response or observation structure, show the boundary of the taught methods, and give authoritative next steps; no full fitting route is promised. |

## Teaching-case contract summary

- **Issue:** #467. **Reader capability:** produce and defend predictions for ecological questions while tracing design and model assumptions to uncertainty.
- **Boundary:** simulated ecological surveys with known generating process, plus independently sourced, documented open ecological observations; sampling frame, taxon, geography, time and permitted reuse must be confirmed with the data. Separate counts, trials, continuous and strictly positive outcomes when needed. Define whether inference concerns sampled sites, a wider population or prediction at new sites.
- **Inputs:** raw observation records, sampling effort, covariates, group identifiers and codebook; simulation parameters and random seed. **Transformation:** check units and missingness, derive response, denominator/offset, group and model matrix, document exclusions and split by sampling unit. **Intermediate:** auditable model-ready table, design matrix and declared estimand. **Computed result:** fitted parameters, uncertainty, simulated checks and predictions. **Reader operation:** compare effect/contrast or predictions on original scale under stated covariates. **Interpretation:** report units, reference level, population, uncertainty and whether marginal or conditional. **Boundary:** deliberate misspecification, clustering, detection or selection can invalidate interpretation; revise the model where possible and rerun comparisons.
- Executable stages, data licences, versions, commands and cross-language comparison tests are planned, not yet implemented. Record source and retrieval date for real data; do not treat simulation truth as real-data truth. The same central input is used across count alternatives; other outcomes and extension-specific mechanisms use explicitly identified companion simulations or independently selected real data. Figures display generating process, link versus response scales, model matrix, likelihood curvature, IRLS, fit versus residuals, simulated versus observed outcomes, and conditional/marginal predictions, with accessible captions and uncertainty.

## Cumulative chapter architecture and example design

The route table specifies obligations, not a limit on chapter count. Organise
those obligations into substantial chapters, avoiding both fragmented headings
and chapters that merely list model names.

1. **Foundations:** ecological questions and observation processes; notation and
   probability refreshers; a compact ordinary-regression chapter covering
   continuous and categorical predictors, contrasts, interactions, assumptions,
   fitted values, residuals and uncertainty.
2. **Constructing and fitting GLMs:** concrete binary and count motivation;
   distribution, mean, predictor and link; exponential-family structure and
   dispersion; likelihood, score and information; IRLS with hand-worked steps,
   numerical safeguards and convergence limits.
3. **Response-specific models:** binary and grouped binomial responses; counts,
   rates and offsets; positive continuous responses and transformation
   comparisons; multinomial and ordinal outcomes. Develop coefficient meaning,
   useful predictions and failure cases in each chapter.
4. **Inference and model assessment:** likelihood and Wald reasoning, confidence
   versus predictive intervals, response-scale contrasts and averaging,
   residuals, calibration, influence, model comparison, regularisation and
   validation matched to site, group and time. Introduce these ideas alongside
   earlier examples before bringing the common workflow together here.
5. **Extending assumptions:** quasi-likelihood and negative binomial models;
   multilevel structure and GLMMs; nonlinear effects and GAMs; zero-inflated and
   hurdle models. Each receives its own substantial chapter with a complete
   worked case and a diagnosis–revision–re-evaluation cycle.
6. **Bayesian construction and computation:** an analytic updating example,
   regression likelihood and priors, prior predictive checking, posterior
   computation and diagnostics, posterior prediction, regularisation and
   sensitivity, and a worked hierarchical extension. Connect back to the
   preceding frequentist models without implying interchangeable intervals.
7. **Synthesis and further study:** complete real-data analyses, model-building
   decisions and communication; limits of the observation design; boundaries
   requiring specialist response, detection, spatial, temporal or survival
   models; exercises with outline solutions and an annotated bibliography.

For each chapter, plan several distinct illustrative devices where useful:
small calculations, diagrams, simulations with known truth, real-data decisions,
comparisons, deliberate failures and exercises. Count examples by the questions
they answer, not by a numerical quota. Do not imply that every new example
needs a different dataset. Main worked analyses have R, Python and Julia
implementations; shorter calculations can use R alone when repetition adds no
understanding. Infeasible software coverage is a planning blocker to resolve,
not permission to silently drop a topic or promised main implementation.
